What JLL Sees This Holiday Season

Insight Analytics·Nov 26, 2026·4 min read
All Insights

JLL’s 2026 Holiday Retail Report indicates a 9.7% year-over-year surge in holiday spending, driven by a structural shift in consumer behavior toward experiential over transactional purchasing. (see Live Markets Dashboard →)

This quantitative uplift signals a fundamental realignment in retail unit economics, where the traditional margin profile of physical goods is being compressed by the rising cost of customer acquisition and the intangible nature of experience-based revenue. For CFOs, the implication is that gross margin assumptions for brick-and-mortar entities must be recalibrated to account for higher service intensity. Unlike standard merchandise sales, which benefit from predictable inventory turnover, experience-driven revenue often carries higher variable costs related to labor and facility utilization. Consequently, operating margin stability will depend less on volume growth and more on the ability to optimize fixed-cost absorption through higher foot traffic density. Boards overseeing retail portfolios should scrutinize whether current capital expenditure plans adequately support the infrastructure required to deliver these high-touch experiences, as under-investment in service capacity can erode the perceived value proposition and, by extension, long-term brand equity.

From a capital allocation perspective, the 9.7% spending increase presents a complex trade-off between immediate liquidity generation and long-term asset valuation. While higher top-line revenue improves free cash flow in the short term, the strategic value of this growth lies in its potential to support multiple expansion if investors perceive the retailer as successfully transitioning into a service-oriented business model. However, this transition requires significant operational efficiency gains to prevent the dilution of EBITDA margins. The cost structure of experiential retail is inherently more labor-intensive and location-dependent, meaning that traditional lean manufacturing or six sigma process improvements applicable to logistics may have limited utility. Instead, the focus must shift to dynamic pricing and inventory optimization for ancillary goods that complement the core experience, ensuring that every square foot of real estate generates maximum return on invested capital.

The shift toward experiences also introduces new dimensions to enterprise risk and supply chain management. Unlike standardized goods, experiential offerings are highly localized and sensitive to regional economic conditions, complicating hedging strategies and liquidity planning. Treasury functions must account for the volatility in cash conversion cycles, as experience-driven sales may not correlate with traditional seasonal inventory peaks. Furthermore, the reliance on physical presence increases exposure to operational disruptions, making business continuity planning a critical component of the ERM framework. The integration of AI in this context is not about automating transactions, but rather enhancing personalization and predictive analytics to drive repeat visitation. The ROI on such AI implementations must be measured against the lifetime value of the customer, rather than immediate transactional gains, reflecting a longer payback period that requires patient capital allocation.

The broader strategic implication is that the retail sector is bifurcating into two distinct asset classes: high-volume, low-margin commodity retailers and high-touch, high-margin experience providers. For boards, the fiduciary duty now includes ensuring that the corporate strategy clearly articulates which of these models the firm is pursuing, as the capital requirements and risk profiles of each are fundamentally different. The 9.7% growth figure is not merely a sales metric but a signal of market segmentation, where consumers are actively reallocating budgets away from discretionary goods toward memorable engagements. This suggests that competitive advantage will increasingly derive from brand loyalty and community integration rather than price competitiveness or supply chain speed. Whether this trend represents a permanent structural shift or a temporary cyclical preference remains an open question, but the immediate pressure on executives is to align their cost structures and capital deployment with the reality that the "product" is now the experience itself, demanding a re-evaluation of how value is created, captured, and sustained in the retail landscape.
