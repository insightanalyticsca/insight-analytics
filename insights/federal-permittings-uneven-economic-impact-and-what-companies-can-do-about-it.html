Federal permitting’s uneven economic impact, and what companies can do about it

Insight Analytics·Oct 28, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

Federal permitting delays create a non-uniform drag on capital efficiency, disproportionately eroding the projected IRR of infrastructure-heavy projects in states with complex regulatory landscapes.

McKinsey’s analysis highlights that the economic impact of federal permitting is not a flat tax on all capital expenditure but a variable cost that distorts ROI calculations across geographies. For CFOs modeling NPV and IRR, the variance in permitting timelines acts as a direct headwind to free cash flow realization. When a project’s timeline extends by months or years, the present value of future cash flows diminishes, effectively lowering the hurdle rate required for investment approval. This unevenness means that two identical projects in different states may yield divergent returns solely due to regulatory friction, complicating capital allocation decisions that rely on standardized internal rates of return. Boards must recognize that permitting risk is not merely an operational nuisance but a material factor in fiduciary duty assessments regarding where to deploy shareholder capital.

From a cost structure perspective, prolonged permitting phases inflate opex through extended project management, legal fees, and idle labor costs, while simultaneously delaying the onset of revenue generation. This creates a working capital strain, as companies must fund pre-revenue activities for longer durations, potentially impacting liquidity ratios and credit ratings if debt-financed. The unit economics of such projects deteriorate as fixed costs are spread over a longer development period, compressing EBITDA margins in the early years of operation. Executives must account for these hidden costs in their run-rate financial models, recognizing that the true cost of entry into certain markets includes a significant regulatory premium that is often underrepresented in traditional budgeting exercises.

The market research implications suggest that investors are increasingly pricing in regulatory risk, leading to multiple compression for firms with heavy exposure to permitting-dependent sectors. As valuation models incorporate higher risk premiums for jurisdictions with unpredictable regulatory environments, companies may face pressure to divest or restructure portfolios to shed high-friction assets. This dynamic influences M&A strategy, where acquirers may discount targets based on their geographic permitting exposure, or where strategic buyers seek assets in jurisdictions with more predictable regulatory pathways to preserve synergies. The strategic implication is that market access is no longer just about demand but about the speed and certainty of regulatory approval, which directly impacts competitive positioning and market sizing assumptions.

The findings suggest that permitting variability is a structural feature of the current economic landscape that will persist, requiring a fundamental shift in how companies approach enterprise risk management. The trade-off between entering high-growth but high-friction markets and staying in lower-growth but stable jurisdictions is likely to sharpen, forcing executives to weigh capital efficiency against market share growth. For boards, the implication is that oversight of regulatory risk must move from a compliance checkbox to a core component of strategic planning, where the cost of delay is quantified alongside the cost of capital. Whether this regulatory friction will eventually lead to broader federal reform or become a permanent differentiator in state-level competitiveness remains an open question, but the immediate impact on profitability and capital allocation is already evident in the divergent performance of geographically diverse portfolios.
