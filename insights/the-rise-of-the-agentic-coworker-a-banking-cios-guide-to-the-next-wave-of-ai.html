The rise of the agentic coworker: A banking CIO’s guide to the next wave of AI

Insight Analytics·Oct 28, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The transition from generative AI chatbots to agentic AI represents a fundamental shift in banking operational economics, where the primary value driver is no longer content generation but the autonomous execution of complex, multi-step workflows that directly impact operating margin and cost-to-serve.

From a corporate governance and enterprise risk perspective, the deployment of agentic systems introduces a new class of fiduciary and compliance challenges that traditional IT oversight structures are ill-equipped to handle. As McKinsey’s analysis highlights, these agents do not merely suggest actions; they execute them across disparate systems. For the board, this elevates the stakes of AI governance from a technical issue to a core component of ERM (Enterprise Risk Management). The risk is no longer just hallucination in a text output, but the potential for an agent to autonomously breach regulatory constraints or execute a transaction that violates internal control policies. Consequently, the audit committee must evolve its oversight framework to account for algorithmic accountability, ensuring that compliance is embedded in the agent’s decision logic rather than applied as a post-hoc check. This requires a redefinition of cyber risk and liquidity risk models, as autonomous agents can move capital or data at speeds that outpace human intervention capabilities.

In terms of cost structure and efficiency, agentic AI targets the highest-volume, lowest-margin segments of the banking value chain, such as loan origination, KYC (Know Your Customer) verification, and routine trade settlement. By automating these end-to-end processes, banks can achieve significant opex reduction, directly improving EBITDA margin. McKinsey posits that the value of these agents lies in their ability to collaborate across workflows, breaking down the silos that traditionally inflate unit economics. This is not merely a labor substitution play; it is a structural optimization of the run-rate cost base. The ROI calculation shifts from simple headcount savings to a broader assessment of capital efficiency, as faster cycle times in lending and trading can accelerate free cash flow generation and improve working capital management.

The AI implementation cost landscape is also undergoing a paradigm shift. While initial capex for model development remains high, the marginal inference cost of agentic operations is expected to decline as models become more specialized and efficient. For capital allocation purposes, this suggests a higher NPV (Net Present Value) for AI initiatives that focus on process automation over those focused solely on customer-facing chatbots. The market research indicates that early adopters who successfully integrate these agents into their core banking infrastructure will likely see multiple expansion as investors re-rate their earnings power based on superior productivity gains and scalable gross margin profiles.

The strategic implication of this shift is that agentic AI is redefining the competitive moat in banking from proprietary data assets to proprietary workflow orchestration. The trade-off for executives is between the speed of deployment and the rigor of governance; moving too quickly risks regulatory sanctions and reputational damage, while moving too slowly cedes market share to competitors who have already optimized their cost structure. Whether this technology generalizes beyond back-office efficiency to core strategic decision-making remains an open question, but the immediate pressure is on board members to ensure that the accountability frameworks for autonomous systems are as robust as the systems themselves. The era of AI as a tool is ending; the era of AI as a coworker, with all the attendant fiduciary and operational complexities, has begun.
