Questioning Your AI ROI? Start With Your AI Strategy

Insight Analytics·Nov 5, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The primary determinant of AI ROI is not the sophistication of the underlying model, but the clarity of the strategic alignment between specific AI use cases and defined financial outcomes. Forrester’s analysis highlights a critical disconnect: while IT departments increasingly bear the burden of escalating inference costs and token consumption, they frequently lack the strategic framework to translate these expenditures into measurable EBITDA improvements, leading to a pervasive crisis of confidence in capital allocation efficiency.

From a cost structure perspective, the current trajectory of AI spending presents a significant risk to operating margin if left unanchored to strategic intent. As Forrester notes, the rapid ramp-up in AI credit usage is driving up opex without a corresponding, visible reduction in run-rate costs or improvement in unit economics. This dynamic creates a scenario where free cash flow is eroded by variable AI costs that are difficult to predict or hedge, placing pressure on treasury functions to manage liquidity against an expanding, uncertain cost base. Without a clear link to process improvement or lean operational gains, the payback period for these investments remains opaque, complicating the NPV calculations that boards rely on for fiduciary oversight.

The ROI challenge is fundamentally a governance and measurement issue, not merely a technical one. Forrester’s engagement data suggests that IT leaders are often tasked with proving value in areas where the AI strategy was never explicitly tied to profitability metrics such as gross margin expansion or EPS impact. This misalignment forces IT to act as a cost center rather than a value driver, creating friction with the CFO’s office. The absence of a robust AI governance framework means that capital efficiency is compromised, as resources are allocated based on technological novelty rather than market sizing or segmentation opportunities that could justify the valuation premium. Consequently, the IRR on AI initiatives remains difficult to substantiate, undermining the credibility of enterprise risk management models that assume predictable returns on digital investments.

The strategic implication extends beyond immediate financials to the broader corporate strategy and M&A landscape. Companies that cannot articulate a clear AI ROI narrative risk multiple compression in the public markets, as investors increasingly discount firms with high, unproven AI spend. Forrester’s findings imply that the lack of a defined strategy is a competitive liability, potentially ceding ground to peers who have successfully integrated AI into their supply chain optimization or logistics networks to improve OTIF rates. This strategic ambiguity also complicates divestiture or portfolio review processes, as the true value of AI-enabled assets remains obscured by the noise of raw cost data, making it difficult to identify genuine synergies in potential deals.

The core tension lies in the transition of AI from an experimental capex line item to a core component of operating leverage. For boards weighing similar moves, the trade-off between rapid adoption and rigorous financial modeling is likely to sharpen as inference costs continue to rise. The implication for capital allocation is that AI spend must be subjected to the same rigorous scrutiny as any other major investment, requiring clear links to margin expansion or cost avoidance. Whether this approach generalizes beyond early adopters remains an open question, but the current data suggests that without a strategy-first approach, AI will remain a source of margin volatility rather than a driver of sustainable value creation. The strategic imperative is to align AI maturity with fiduciary duties, ensuring that every dollar spent on AI credits contributes to a defensible, quantifiable advantage in the market.
