Charting the US-China Trade War: What Does 'Made in Vietnam' Mean?

Insight Analytics·Oct 17, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The strategic imperative for North American manufacturers is no longer merely to reduce tariff exposure, but to rigorously validate the provenance integrity of their supply chains, as recent trade microdata analysis suggests that while "Made in Vietnam" labels are proliferating, the degree of actual value-added transformation often falls short of the threshold required to legitimately bypass US tariffs.

From a corporate governance and compliance perspective, the reliance on transshipment hubs creates significant fiduciary risk. Boards must recognize that labeling goods as Vietnamese-origin when the substantive manufacturing remains in China constitutes a potential violation of trade laws, exposing the firm to retroactive duties, penalties, and reputational damage. This is not merely a logistical issue; it is a governance failure that undermines the ESG and ethical sourcing commitments made to stakeholders. The board oversight function must therefore extend beyond traditional financial controls to include robust supply chain due diligence, ensuring that the compliance architecture can withstand scrutiny from both US Customs and Border Protection and international trade partners.

In terms of cost structure and efficiency, the perceived savings from routing through Vietnam are often illusory when accounting for the increased complexity of logistics and inventory optimization. While the headline tariff rate may appear lower, the run-rate costs of managing a multi-jurisdictional supply chain—encompassing higher freight costs, longer lead times, and elevated working capital requirements—can erode the gross margin advantage. Executives must evaluate the unit economics of these routes with precision, recognizing that the operating margin impact of supply chain volatility often outweighs the static tariff differential. The ROI on such strategies is contingent on the ability to maintain OTIF (On-Time In-Full) performance, which is frequently compromised by the added layers of intermediation.

From a market research and valuation standpoint, investors are increasingly scrutinizing the capital allocation decisions that underpin these supply chain shifts. If a company’s free cash flow projections rely on tariff avoidance that is legally precarious, the multiple expansion associated with its stock price may be unsustainable. The TAM (Total Addressable Market) for compliant, high-value-added manufacturing in Southeast Asia is real, but it requires significant capex investment in local infrastructure and workforce development, not just the movement of finished goods. The NPV of these investments depends on the durability of the strategic positioning, which is threatened if the "Made in Vietnam" label is viewed by policymakers as a loophole rather than a genuine shift in production.

The broader implication for executive leadership is that the distinction between nearshoring and transshipment is becoming a critical determinant of long-term competitive position. The findings suggest that the strategic value of Southeast Asian manufacturing lies not in tariff arbitrage, but in genuine process improvement and local value creation. For boards weighing similar moves, the trade-off between short-term margin relief and long-term regulatory risk is likely to sharpen as US policymakers refine their enforcement mechanisms. Whether this approach generalizes beyond specific product categories remains an open question, but the trend indicates a shift toward a more rigorous, data-driven assessment of supply chain authenticity. The ultimate lesson is that in an era of heightened geopolitical tension, the integrity of the supply chain is as much a financial asset as it is a logistical one, and its valuation must reflect the true cost of compliance and resilience.
