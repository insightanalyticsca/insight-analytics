P&G’s shift to an open-innovation model yielded a 60% increase in R&D productivity, demonstrating that external sourcing can outperform traditional internal development on both speed and cost efficiency.

From a capital allocation and ROI perspective, the Harvard Business Review’s analysis of Procter & Gamble’s "Connect and Develop" strategy challenges the long-standing assumption that proprietary R&D is the sole driver of sustainable growth. Under the previous "invent it ourselves" model, P&G faced diminishing returns, where escalating R&D spend generated smaller payoffs, effectively eroding operating margin potential. By pivoting to a model that systematically sources proven technologies from suppliers, competitors, and entrepreneurs, P&G optimized its capital efficiency. The firm leveraged its existing scale and marketing capabilities to refine and commercialize external ideas, thereby reducing the payback period on innovation investments. This approach allowed P&G to launch over 100 new products in two years, with key contributors like Olay Regenerist and Swiffer Dusters driving significant top-line growth without the proportional increase in fixed capex associated with expanding internal research facilities.

The strategic implications extend to cost structure and supply chain optimization. By integrating external partners into the development lifecycle, P&G effectively decentralized the risk and cost of early-stage innovation. This model reduces the run-rate of internal R&D overhead while maintaining control over final product quality and brand integrity. For CFOs, this represents a shift from a fixed-cost innovation model to a more variable, performance-based structure. The ability to "connect" with a global network of scientists and entrepreneurs allows for a more agile response to market shifts, minimizing the inventory risk associated with slow-moving, internally developed products that fail to gain traction. This agility is critical in maintaining EBITDA stability, as it prevents the accumulation of sunk costs in failed internal projects.

Furthermore, this approach has significant governance and competitive positioning implications. The traditional "four walls" model creates a siloed innovation environment that is increasingly unsustainable in a hyper-competitive global market. HBR’s research suggests that companies clinging to this model risk multiple compression as investors discount their growth prospects due to perceived inefficiencies. By embracing open innovation, P&G demonstrated a more robust Enterprise Risk Management framework, diversifying its source of innovation to mitigate the risk of technological obsolescence. This strategy also enhances market research capabilities, as the firm gains access to a broader spectrum of consumer insights and technological trends through its external network, allowing for more precise TAM (Total Addressable Market) identification and segmentation.

The broader analytical context suggests that the "Connect and Develop" model is not merely a tactical sourcing strategy but a fundamental redefinition of corporate strategy and M&A logic. It implies that the highest NPV opportunities often lie in licensing, partnership, and acquisition of existing technologies rather than in the high-risk, high-cost pursuit of net-new invention. For boards weighing similar moves, the trade-off between maintaining strict intellectual property control and achieving rapid free cash flow generation is likely to sharpen. The findings indicate that the unit economics of innovation are improving when firms leverage external networks, but this requires a cultural shift away from internal pride in invention toward a pragmatic focus on commercialization speed. Whether this approach generalizes beyond consumer goods into capital-intensive industries remains an open question, but the data from P&G suggests that the era of self-contained R&D dominance is yielding to a more collaborative, networked model of value creation.
