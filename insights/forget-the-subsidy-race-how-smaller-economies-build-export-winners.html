Forget the subsidy race: How smaller economies build export winners

Insight Analytics·Oct 30, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The strategic imperative for mid-sized economies is no longer to match the capital intensity of the US or China, but to engineer comparative advantage through targeted industrial policy that yields higher ROI on limited public and private capital.

IMD Business School’s analysis challenges the prevailing narrative that smaller nations must engage in a subsidy race to remain competitive. Instead, the research highlights five export winners, including Indonesia and Chile, that have successfully decoupled their growth from massive fiscal outlays. For the CFO, this shifts the focus from absolute spend to capital efficiency. The implication is that EBITDA generation in these sectors is driven not by volume alone, but by the precision of capital allocation toward high-value nodes in the global supply chain. By avoiding the trap of broad-based subsidies, these economies preserve fiscal liquidity, allowing for more sustainable free cash flow generation that can be reinvested into infrastructure and human capital rather than being consumed by short-term stimulus.

From a supply chain optimization perspective, the success of these five economies underscores the value of network design over raw scale. Indonesia’s approach to nickel processing, for instance, demonstrates how controlling upstream resources allows for greater leverage over gross margin and pricing power in downstream battery materials. This is a classic case of vertical integration creating unit economics that are resilient to global price volatility. For CEOs in resource-intensive industries, the lesson is that operating margin expansion often comes from securing critical inputs rather than merely optimizing labor costs. The logistics and inventory management strategies employed by these nations suggest that OTIF (On-Time In-Full) performance is a function of strategic location and regulatory stability, not just technological investment.

The governance and risk management dimensions are equally critical. By avoiding the subsidy race, these smaller economies mitigate the sovereign credit risk that often accompanies heavy fiscal borrowing. For boards overseeing multinational operations, this stability reduces the enterprise risk associated with operating in jurisdictions prone to policy whiplash. The treasury function benefits from predictable regulatory environments, which lowers the cost of hedging and improves working capital cycles. Furthermore, the ESG compliance landscape in these countries is increasingly aligned with global standards, reducing the compliance overhead for foreign investors. This creates a valuation multiple premium for assets located in stable, well-governed emerging markets, as investors discount the risk of expropriation or sudden policy shifts.

The findings suggest that the future of industrial strategy for mid-sized economies lies in portfolio management of their export base, treating national industries like a diversified investment portfolio. The trade-off between short-term fiscal stimulus and long-term structural competitiveness is likely to sharpen as global capital becomes more selective. For executives weighing M&A opportunities in these regions, the implication is that synergies are more likely to be realized through operational integration and process improvement rather than through market share acquisition alone. Whether this model generalizes beyond the five highlighted cases remains an open question, but the core insight is clear: capital discipline and strategic focus are more potent drivers of export success than sheer scale. The broader business context is one where efficiency and resilience are the new currencies of competitive advantage, replacing the old paradigm of scale and subsidy.
