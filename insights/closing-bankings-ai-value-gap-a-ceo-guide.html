Closing banking’s AI value gap: A CEO guide

Insight Analytics·Nov 1, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

McKinsey’s analysis indicates that the primary barrier to banking AI value is not technological capability, but the absence of an operating model that converts discrete AI investments into reusable, enterprise-wide "AI capital."

From a profitability and margin perspective, the current trajectory of AI adoption in financial services is creating a significant divergence between input costs and realized EBITDA impact. While capex and opex associated with model development and infrastructure continue to rise, the unit economics of individual AI applications often fail to scale because they remain siloed. McKinsey’s framework suggests that without a centralized mechanism to standardize and reuse AI assets, banks are effectively paying for custom software development rather than building a scalable platform. This results in a gross margin compression where the marginal cost of deploying new AI features remains high, preventing the operating leverage that typically characterizes mature digital transformations. The ROI on these isolated projects is therefore constrained, as the payback period extends beyond standard capital allocation thresholds due to the lack of cross-functional utility.

The AI implementation cost structure reveals a critical inefficiency in how banks approach AI governance and deployment. When AI solutions are treated as one-off projects rather than components of a unified AI maturity strategy, the inference cost and maintenance burden are duplicated across departments. This fragmentation undermines capital efficiency, as the same foundational models are rebuilt or re-optimized for different use cases, such as credit risk assessment versus fraud detection. For the CFO, this represents a misallocation of resources where free cash flow is consumed by redundant technical debt rather than being reinvested in high-yield strategic initiatives. The absence of a shared "AI capital" pool means that the NPV of the overall AI portfolio is lower than the sum of its parts, as synergies are never realized.

From a corporate governance and enterprise risk standpoint, the siloed approach to AI introduces significant compliance and cyber risk exposures. Without a board-level oversight framework that treats AI as a core asset class, fiduciary duties regarding risk management are compromised. The ERM (Enterprise Risk Management) function struggles to monitor the systemic risks of disparate AI models, potentially leading to regulatory penalties or reputational damage that erodes credit rating stability. Furthermore, the lack of standardized AI governance protocols hampers the ability to demonstrate responsible AI usage to regulators and investors, which can lead to multiple compression in valuation metrics as the market prices in higher operational risk. The board must view AI not merely as a technology initiative but as a strategic asset requiring rigorous fiduciary oversight to ensure that the value gap between investment and return is closed.

The strategic implication is that the value of AI in banking is increasingly determined by architectural coherence rather than model sophistication. For boards weighing similar moves, the trade-off between rapid, decentralized experimentation and centralized, standardized deployment is likely to sharpen, with the latter offering superior capital efficiency and risk mitigation. The findings suggest that the competitive advantage will accrue to institutions that can treat AI as a reusable asset class, thereby lowering the run-rate cost of innovation and enhancing operating margin. Whether this approach generalizes beyond large-scale banking operations remains an open question, but the principle that AI capital must be managed with the same rigor as financial capital is becoming a defining factor in long-term shareholder value creation.
