Evaluating the revenue potential of energy storage technologies

Insight Analytics·Oct 27, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The primary valuation error in the energy sector’s capital planning is the systematic undervaluation of energy storage assets, a miscalculation that distorts NPV and IRR metrics by ignoring the full spectrum of revenue streams available to flexible infrastructure.

McKinsey’s analysis highlights that traditional financial models often fail to capture the true revenue potential of storage technologies because they rely on static assumptions rather than dynamic market participation. For CFOs and treasury teams, this oversight creates a significant gap in capital allocation efficiency. When storage is evaluated solely on its ability to arbitrage peak and off-peak electricity prices, the resulting unit economics appear marginal. However, when the model is adjusted to include ancillary services, capacity payments, and grid stability premiums, the EBITDA margin profile improves substantially. This shift in perspective is critical for boards assessing the fiduciary duty to maximize shareholder value, as the current conservative estimates may be leading to the rejection of projects with robust underlying free cash flow generation potential.

From a cost structure perspective, the integration of advanced analytics into storage operations transforms the asset from a passive battery into an active revenue generator. The source suggests that investors must adjust their evaluation approach to account for the operational complexity required to capture these diverse revenue streams. This implies a higher initial capex for sophisticated control systems and software, but the long-term run-rate opex is offset by the increased gross margin derived from optimized dispatch. For COOs overseeing operational efficiency, this represents a move toward process improvement where the asset’s value is not just in its energy density, but in its algorithmic ability to navigate volatile market conditions. The ROI calculation must therefore reflect the premium paid for intelligent management systems, which serve as the bridge between raw hardware and profitable market participation.

The implications for market research and valuation are profound, particularly as the TAM for grid-scale storage expands. McKinsey’s findings indicate that the current market pricing of these assets does not reflect their full utility, suggesting a potential for multiple expansion as the industry matures and standardized valuation frameworks emerge. For M&A strategists, this creates a bifurcated landscape: assets with robust, data-driven revenue models will command higher premiums, while those relying on legacy, static valuation methods will face multiple compression. This divergence impacts enterprise risk management, as the volatility of revenue streams requires more sophisticated hedging strategies and liquidity management. Boards must ensure that their ESG reporting and compliance frameworks accurately reflect the operational reality of these assets, avoiding the risk of greenwashing accusations if the sustainability benefits are overstated relative to the actual financial performance.

The strategic implication is that the barrier to entry in energy storage is shifting from hardware acquisition to analytical capability. The trade-off for executives is between the short-term cost of implementing complex, AI-driven optimization platforms and the long-term security of diversified revenue streams. Whether this approach generalizes beyond mature markets into emerging grids remains an open question, but the core finding stands: the financial case for storage is stronger than current market consensus suggests, provided that the valuation model captures the full complexity of grid services. This re-evaluation is not merely a technical adjustment but a fundamental rethinking of how flexible assets contribute to corporate profitability and strategic positioning in a decarbonizing energy landscape.
