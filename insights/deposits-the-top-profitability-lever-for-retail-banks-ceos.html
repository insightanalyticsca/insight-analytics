Deposits: The top profitability lever for retail banks’ CEOs

Insight Analytics·Oct 22, 2026·4 min read
All Insights

McKinsey’s analysis identifies deposit management as the single most potent lever for retail bank CEOs to expand net interest margins and stabilize free cash flow in a volatile rate environment.

From a treasury and enterprise risk perspective, the traditional view of deposits as a passive liability is fundamentally flawed. McKinsey’s research underscores that sophisticated deposit management directly influences liquidity coverage ratios and funding costs, which are critical inputs for any bank’s economic value of equity. For the CFO, this means that the cost of funds is not a static market variable but a managed outcome. By actively steering deposit mix and pricing, banks can mitigate the risk of credit rating downgrades associated with high wholesale funding reliance. The board’s fiduciary duty to ensure long-term solvency is best served by treating deposit behavior as a core component of the Enterprise Risk Management framework, rather than a back-office operational detail. This shift requires rigorous oversight of how customer segmentation impacts working capital stability, ensuring that the bank’s liability profile remains resilient against sudden shifts in consumer or corporate cash flows.

In terms of profitability and margin, the ability to lower the cost of deposits translates directly into an expansion of EBITDA margin and EPS. McKinsey notes that even modest improvements in deposit pricing efficiency can yield significant operating leverage, as these savings flow through to the bottom line with minimal incremental opex. This is particularly relevant for retail banks facing pressure from multiple compression in public markets, where demonstrating consistent unit economics improvement is essential to maintaining valuation. The analytical lens here is one of capital efficiency: by reducing reliance on expensive wholesale funding, banks can optimize their return on equity without requiring additional equity capital injections. This dynamic is crucial for boards evaluating capital allocation strategies, as it suggests that internal margin expansion through deposit optimization may offer a higher IRR than external growth initiatives in the current macroeconomic climate.

The cost structure and efficiency implications are equally profound, particularly regarding the integration of AI implementation in customer analytics. McKinsey’s insight suggests that advanced data models can predict customer price sensitivity and churn risk, allowing for dynamic, personalized pricing that maximizes gross margin without eroding customer loyalty. This represents a shift from static, one-size-fits-all pricing to a process improvement model that leverages inference cost efficiencies to drive revenue. For the COO, this is a matter of run-rate optimization: the initial capex required for these analytical platforms must be weighed against the sustained productivity gain in funding cost reduction. The ROI on such AI maturity investments is often underestimated because the value is realized in the treasury function rather than directly in the sales channel, creating a disconnect that governance structures must bridge to ensure adequate funding for these high-value analytical capabilities.

The strategic implications of this shift extend beyond immediate financial metrics, touching on the broader corporate strategy of retail banking. The findings suggest that the competitive moat for retail banks is increasingly defined by the sophistication of their liability management rather than their asset side. For boards weighing similar moves, the trade-off between short-term customer acquisition costs and long-term funding stability is likely to sharpen. The implication for capital allocation is that resources should be directed toward enhancing the analytical infrastructure that supports deposit intelligence, as this capability provides a durable advantage in a market where TAM expansion is limited by demographic and regulatory constraints. Whether this approach generalizes beyond large retail banks to smaller community institutions remains an open question, but the core principle holds: in an era of constrained growth, the most reliable path to free cash flow generation lies in the disciplined, data-driven optimization of the existing customer base’s funding behavior.
