The view from the Moon: How to rethink your global strategy

Insight Analytics·Oct 25, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The erosion of the product-service boundary is forcing a fundamental re-evaluation of global capital allocation, shifting the primary driver of EBITDA margin from static scale efficiencies to dynamic ecosystem orchestration.

IMD Business School’s strategic analysis, led by Yoshinori Fujikawa, posits that the traditional siloed approach to global strategy is obsolete as the distinction between tangible products and intangible services dissolves. For the CFO, this blurring represents a critical shift in unit economics and gross margin structures. Historically, hardware or physical goods offered predictable, albeit compressed, margins, while services commanded higher premiums but required significant human capital. The new paradigm demands a hybrid model where value is captured through continuous service integration, requiring a re-examination of operating margin targets. If a company’s P&L continues to treat these streams as distinct, it risks mispricing its offerings and failing to capture the full lifetime value of the customer relationship, thereby suppressing EPS potential in mature markets.

From a corporate governance and board oversight perspective, the "shift, melt, and tilt" framework implies that the board’s fiduciary duty now extends to overseeing the integration of disparate business units. The "melt" phase, where capabilities are pooled, creates complex internal transfer pricing challenges that require rigorous compliance and audit scrutiny to ensure no value leakage occurs between divisions. Furthermore, the "tilt" phase, which involves reorienting the organization toward specific ecosystem opportunities, necessitates a robust ERM framework. Boards must ensure that the pursuit of these new, fluid revenue streams does not expose the firm to unquantified cyber risk or reputational risk, particularly as data flows across borders and partners. The fiduciary responsibility is to verify that the governance structure can handle the increased velocity of decision-making without compromising control.

The ROI and capital allocation implications are profound, as the "shift" phase often requires significant capex in digital infrastructure and opex in talent acquisition before tangible returns materialize. This creates a tension for the treasury function, which must balance the need for liquidity to fund these strategic pivots against the pressure to maintain a strong credit rating and free cash flow generation. The payback period for investments in ecosystem orchestration is typically longer and more variable than traditional lean or six sigma process improvements, requiring a more nuanced NPV model that accounts for optionality and network effects. Executives must recognize that the cost structure is no longer just about reducing run-rate expenses but about investing in the innovation capabilities that allow the firm to "tilt" toward high-growth segments.

The strategic implications of this framework suggest that the competitive advantage of the future lies not in vertical integration, but in the ability to orchestrate external capabilities seamlessly. For boards weighing similar moves, the trade-off between maintaining strict control over core competencies and the agility required to "melt" into broader ecosystems is likely to sharpen. The implication for capital allocation is that traditional multiple expansion driven by stable, predictable cash flows may give way to valuation based on the firm’s ability to generate synergies across diverse, interconnected value streams. Whether this approach generalizes beyond technology-heavy industries remains an open question, but the directional pressure is clear: the firm of the future is less a monolithic entity and more a dynamic node in a global network, where market sizing and segmentation are defined by capability flows rather than geographic or product boundaries. This shift demands a fundamental rethinking of how strategic value is measured and governed.
