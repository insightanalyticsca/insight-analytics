Radical federalism: The way to tackle the AI velocity problem

Insight Analytics·Oct 18, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The central governance challenge for C-suite leaders is that rigid, top-down AI control structures are increasingly failing to capture the velocity of value creation, suggesting that a shift toward radical federalism—a decentralized, unit-focused hybrid model—may be necessary to protect operating margin and accelerate AI ROI.

IMD Business School’s analysis of the "AI velocity problem" argues that traditional centralized planning creates bottlenecks that stifle the rapid iteration required for modern AI deployment. From a corporate governance perspective, this challenges the board’s fiduciary duty to oversee risk without stifling innovation. When AI initiatives are subjected to a single, monolithic approval process, the time-to-value extends significantly, eroding the competitive advantage of early adoption. The research suggests that excessive centralization leads to a form of bureaucratic inertia where the cost of compliance and oversight outweighs the productivity gains derived from the technology, effectively compressing EBITDA potential in high-growth segments.

From a cost structure & efficiency lens, the federalist approach redefines how opex is allocated across the enterprise. Instead of a centralized AI center of excellence absorbing all development costs, business units bear the responsibility for their specific AI use cases, aligning unit economics directly with local value creation. This shifts the burden of AI implementation cost to the line of business, ensuring that only initiatives with clear payback periods and positive NPV proceed. For CFOs, this model improves capital efficiency by preventing the diversion of resources into low-impact, centrally mandated projects that lack direct ties to revenue growth or gross margin expansion. The result is a more disciplined allocation of free cash flow, where investment is driven by local market realities rather than corporate-wide standardization.

The strategic implication for market positioning is significant. In fast-moving sectors, the ability to deploy AI solutions at the speed of market demand is a key differentiator. A centralized model often results in a "one-size-fits-all" approach that fails to address specific segmentation needs, whereas a federalist structure allows for tailored solutions that enhance customer experience and retention. This agility can lead to multiple expansion as investors recognize the company’s ability to scale AI-driven efficiencies across diverse business units without the drag of centralized bureaucracy. The source emphasizes that this hybrid approach outperforms both chaotic, unregulated adoption and rigid central control, striking a balance that preserves enterprise risk management while maximizing inference cost optimization at the unit level.

The findings suggest that the strategic trade-off for boards is no longer between control and chaos, but between the speed of value capture and the rigor of oversight. For executives, this implies a fundamental restructuring of AI governance frameworks, moving from command-and-control to a model of empowered autonomy with clear guardrails. The second-order effect is a cultural shift where AI maturity is measured not by the number of deployed models, but by the velocity at which business units can iterate and realize operating margin improvements. Whether this federalist approach generalizes beyond technology-led industries remains an open question, but the pressure to align capital allocation with local market dynamics is likely to intensify as AI becomes a core component of competitive strategy rather than a peripheral experiment. The ultimate test will be whether decentralized units can maintain sufficient cyber risk and compliance standards without the buffer of central oversight, a tension that will define the next phase of AI-driven corporate transformation.
