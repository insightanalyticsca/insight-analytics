What CEOs don’t hear and why it matters

Insight Analytics·Oct 16, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The erosion of candor within executive suites represents a critical governance failure that directly compromises fiduciary oversight, as the absence of unfiltered feedback distorts the risk assessment and strategic calibration required for sustainable EBITDA growth. IMD Business School research highlights that feedback is fundamentally relational rather than merely a communicative channel, suggesting that when organizational structures suppress honest dialogue, the resulting information asymmetry creates a blind spot in enterprise risk management that traditional compliance mechanisms fail to detect. This is not merely a cultural concern; it is a structural defect in the board’s ability to monitor management performance and strategic execution.

From a corporate governance perspective, the board’s primary duty is to ensure that information flows to the top are accurate and unvarnished. When candor is stifled by hierarchical friction or fear of reprisal, the board receives a sanitized version of operational reality, leading to delayed recognition of emerging threats. This opacity increases the likelihood of strategic missteps, as decisions are made on incomplete data, potentially exposing the firm to significant reputational and regulatory liabilities. The IMD analysis suggests that the cost of this silence is not just intangible; it manifests in missed opportunities for process improvement and delayed corrective actions that would otherwise preserve operating margin.

The financial implications of poor feedback loops extend to capital allocation and ROI metrics. When executives do not hear the truth about project viability or market shifts, capital is often misallocated to underperforming initiatives, depressing free cash flow and NPV. In the context of AI implementation, for example, if frontline teams do not candidly report the high inference costs or low productivity gains of new tools, the organization may continue to scale inefficient AI spend, eroding gross margin. IMD’s focus on the relational nature of feedback implies that fixing these channels is a prerequisite for accurate unit economics modeling and effective cost structure optimization. Without this clarity, lean and six sigma initiatives remain superficial, failing to address the root causes of inefficiency.

Furthermore, the lack of candor distorts market research and strategic positioning. If internal stakeholders are reluctant to share negative insights regarding customer segmentation or competitive dynamics, the firm’s TAM estimates may be overly optimistic, leading to aggressive M&A strategies based on flawed synergy projections. This misalignment between internal reality and external strategy can result in multiple compression as the market realizes the disconnect between reported performance and actual operational health. The IMD research underscores that the "candor cost"—the perceived social or political price of speaking truth—is a tangible barrier to strategic agility.

The strategic implication of these findings is that candor must be treated as a core component of governance infrastructure, not merely a soft-skill development area. For boards weighing the trade-offs between short-term EPS pressure and long-term resilience, the data suggests that investing in relational feedback mechanisms yields a higher IRR than traditional compliance spending. The second-order effect of restored candor is a more accurate ERM framework, where risks are identified earlier and mitigated at a lower run-rate cost. Whether this relational approach generalizes across all industry verticals remains an open question, but the evidence points to a clear correlation between open dialogue and the precision of capital allocation. Ultimately, the board’s role is to ensure that the cost of silence is no longer borne by shareholders, recognizing that the integrity of information flow is as critical to value creation as the integrity of the balance sheet.
