Why sustainability leaders need to think like change managers

Insight Analytics·Oct 19, 2026·4 min read
All Insights

The primary driver of sustainability ROI is not technological adoption, but the organizational change management required to embed new behaviors into operational run-rates, a factor often underestimated in capital allocation models.

From a corporate governance perspective, board oversight of ESG initiatives has traditionally focused on compliance and reporting metrics, yet IMD Business School’s analysis suggests that fiduciary duty now extends to the human capital dimension of sustainability. The source argues that leaders must understand colleagues’ motivations to bridge the gap between strategic ambition and daily practice. For the board, this implies that ESG risk is no longer solely a regulatory or reputational issue, but an execution risk rooted in organizational inertia. If the workforce does not internalize the sustainability mandate, the compliance costs associated with manual reporting and audit will remain elevated, eroding the potential operating margin benefits of efficiency gains. The governance implication is that sustainability KPIs must be linked to individual and team performance metrics, ensuring that the fiduciary duty to shareholders includes the disciplined execution of sustainability targets rather than just their public declaration.

In terms of cost structure and efficiency, the failure to manage the human element of change often results in a "shadow cost" that distorts unit economics. When employees resist new sustainable processes—whether in supply chain logistics or energy consumption—the organization incurs hidden opex through rework, inefficiency, and the need for constant managerial intervention. IMD’s framework highlights that equipping people to act is as critical as the technology itself. Without this human alignment, the payback period for sustainability investments, such as capex on renewable energy or circular economy infrastructure, can extend significantly beyond projected timelines. This delays the positive impact on EBITDA and free cash flow, as the initial outlay is not offset by immediate operational savings. The analyst view is that sustainability projects should be evaluated not just on their environmental impact, but on their ability to streamline process improvement initiatives, thereby reducing the run-rate of waste and inefficiency.

Regarding ROI and capital allocation, investors are increasingly scrutinizing the NPV of sustainability initiatives, but the calculation is often flawed if it ignores the cost of change management. IMD’s insight that leaders must overcome barriers to scale suggests that the IRR of a sustainability program is contingent on the speed of adoption. If the organization fails to equip its people to act, the capital efficiency of the investment drops, as assets sit underutilized or processes remain suboptimal. This has direct implications for valuation multiples; companies that demonstrate a mature, human-centric approach to sustainability may command a premium, as they signal lower execution risk and higher long-term profitability. Conversely, those that treat sustainability as a purely technical or compliance exercise risk multiple compression as the market prices in the likelihood of stalled execution.

The strategic implication is that sustainability is fundamentally a change management discipline, not just an environmental one. For executives, this reframes the debate from "how much do we spend on green tech" to "how do we restructure our organizational capabilities to sustain these changes." The trade-off is clear: short-term investment in change management and human capital development is necessary to unlock the long-term margin expansion and risk mitigation that sustainability promises. Whether this approach generalizes across all industries remains an open question, but the evidence suggests that the board’s role must evolve to oversee the human architecture of sustainability, ensuring that fiduciary responsibilities are met through both technological and behavioral transformation. The broader context is that in a market where ESG is a key differentiator, the ability to manage change effectively becomes a core competitive advantage, influencing not just operating performance but also market positioning and stakeholder trust.
