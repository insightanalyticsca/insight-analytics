Ann Berry on the Importance of Learning AI

Insight Analytics·Nov 7, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The strategic imperative for board-level oversight has shifted from passive AI adoption to active AI literacy, a competency gap that now directly correlates with fiduciary duty and capital allocation efficiency.

From a corporate governance perspective, the trajectory of leaders like Ann Berry—who transitioned from Goldman Sachs M&A to managing Threadneedle through the pandemic—illustrates a critical evolution in board composition. The traditional reliance on financial acumen alone is insufficient for navigating the current deepfake detection and generative AI landscape. Boards are increasingly tasked with overseeing AI governance frameworks that mitigate cyber risk and reputational exposure, requiring directors to possess a functional understanding of model limitations and data integrity. This shift implies that board refreshment strategies must now prioritize candidates with demonstrated AI maturity and technical fluency, not just sector expertise, to ensure that strategic decisions regarding digital transformation are grounded in realistic operational capabilities rather than speculative hype.

The ROI and capital allocation implications of this learning curve are substantial, particularly for firms operating in the hospitality services and broader service economy sectors. Early-stage bets on AI and deepfake detection represent a new category of opex that must be evaluated against tangible productivity gains and cost structure improvements. Unlike traditional capex investments with clear payback periods, AI initiatives often exhibit non-linear value curves where initial inference costs are high but marginal costs decline rapidly with scale. Executives must therefore apply rigorous NPV and IRR modeling to these intangible assets, recognizing that the unit economics of AI-driven customer service or risk management can fundamentally alter EBITDA margin profiles. The ability to distinguish between experimental AI pilots and scalable, revenue-generating deployments is a key determinant of free cash flow generation in the mid-term.

Furthermore, the expanding IPO landscape and the broader M&A environment are being reshaped by the valuation premiums attached to AI-native business models. As Threadneedle and similar firms navigate portfolio review and potential divestitures, the market sizing and TAM calculations for AI-enabled services are undergoing significant multiple expansion. Investors are increasingly discounting cash flows based on the perceived durability of AI-driven competitive advantages, which creates a bifurcated market where firms with robust AI implementation strategies command higher valuation multiples. This dynamic forces a re-evaluation of synergies in deals, where the integration of AI capabilities becomes a primary driver of strategic value rather than a secondary benefit. The treasury function must also adapt, as enterprise risk management now includes the volatility of AI-related working capital and the potential for credit rating adjustments based on technological obsolescence risk.

The synthesis of these factors suggests that AI literacy is no longer a soft skill but a hard constraint on strategic optionality. For boards weighing similar moves, the trade-off between speed of adoption and governance rigor is likely to sharpen, as the cost of AI misalignment or data breach exceeds the opportunity cost of delayed deployment. The implication for capital allocation is that resources will increasingly flow toward firms that can demonstrate AI governance as a core component of their risk management framework, rather than treating it as an IT afterthought. Whether this approach generalizes beyond early adopters like Berry remains an open question, but the directional pressure is clear: the competitive position of any firm will be defined by its ability to convert AI knowledge into sustainable margin and resilient cash flow. The era of viewing AI as a purely technical challenge is ending; it is now a fundamental board-level strategic variable.
