We’re human beings, not human doings: Why always-on culture weakens productivity

Insight Analytics·Oct 26, 2026·3 min read
All Insights

The erosion of genuine value creation through "permanent activation" cultures is a structural inefficiency that directly compresses operating margins by decoupling visible activity from actual output.

From a cost structure and efficiency perspective, the phenomenon described by IMD Business School represents a critical failure in process optimization. When leadership conflates availability with impact, organizations inadvertently subsidize low-value labor hours, inflating opex without a corresponding increase in EBITDA. This dynamic mirrors the inefficiencies identified in lean manufacturing principles, where non-value-added activities are systematically removed to improve unit economics. In a service-heavy enterprise, the "always-on" model acts as a hidden tax on productivity, where the marginal cost of an additional hour of employee presence exceeds the marginal revenue generated, thereby degrading overall operating margin. The analytical lens here is not merely cultural but financial: it is a measurable drag on run-rate efficiency that standard performance metrics often fail to capture because they track input (hours logged) rather than output (value delivered).

Viewed through the ROI and capital allocation framework, the persistence of this culture signals a misallocation of human capital, a key component of free cash flow generation. Gartner’s broader research on workforce productivity emphasizes that digital transformation investments yield returns only when paired with process re-engineering; without this, technology merely accelerates inefficient workflows. If an organization continues to fund a workforce engaged in permanent activation, it is effectively paying for busywork rather than strategic execution. This has direct implications for capital efficiency, as the cost of labor becomes a fixed, high-burn variable that does not scale with revenue growth. For CFOs, this suggests that the payback period on talent acquisition and retention strategies is being artificially extended by the absence of clear boundaries between work and rest, leading to higher turnover costs and lower net present value of the human capital asset.

The corporate governance and board oversight angle reveals a deeper fiduciary risk. Boards tasked with monitoring ESG compliance and long-term sustainability are increasingly scrutinizing the "S" in ESG, specifically employee well-being and burnout. A culture of permanent activation creates a latent enterprise risk profile that can trigger regulatory scrutiny or reputational damage, impacting credit rating and investor confidence. McKinsey’s analysis on organizational health posits that sustainable performance requires a balance between intensity and recovery; ignoring this balance exposes the firm to liquidity risks associated with sudden, high-volume attrition. Furthermore, in the context of AI implementation, the value of AI spend is contingent on human oversight and strategic direction. If the human layer is exhausted and disconnected from strategic intent, the productivity gain from AI maturity is diminished, as the technology is deployed into a chaotic, reactive environment rather than a structured, efficient one.

The strategic implication of these findings is that the boundary between work and rest is no longer a soft HR issue but a hard financial variable affecting market valuation. For boards weighing the trade-off between short-term EPS pressure and long-term competitive position, the data suggests that multiple compression may occur if investors perceive the organization’s growth model as reliant on unsustainable labor intensity rather than structural efficiency. The second-order effect is a potential valuation discount, as the market prices in the risk of operational fragility. Whether this dynamic generalizes across all sectors remains an open question, but for knowledge-intensive industries, the correlation between genuine value creation and sustainable productivity is likely to become a primary driver of portfolio review and capital allocation decisions in the coming fiscal cycles.
